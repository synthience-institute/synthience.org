One Essay, Many Checkers: How a Field Note Was Written and Verified With Several AI Systems

PG-017 October 4, 2026 Thomas W. Gantz

A worked example of checking AI-assisted writing: what each reviewer caught, what the reviewers got wrong, and what only the human could decide.

In the drafts of the Institute’s Field Note on the AI capture fight, one sentence said that Anthropic’s IPO prospectus warns that “its models” could pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity. The underlying Reuters report says the prospectus warns that “advanced AI” could. (One outlet’s headline said “its AI”; the Reuters text does not.) The difference matters: one is a company saying its own product might end the world, the other is a company describing the field it works in. The wrong version sat in eight successive drafts and passed at least six reviews by AI models from two companies. It was finally caught twice in one day: by a checker comparing every sentence of the essay with its source, and by a fresh adversarial review.

That is the case for this guide in one example. Review by AI systems catches a great deal, and it also misses things, adds its own errors, and changes the writing in ways nobody intends. This guide walks through how FN-018, “The AI Capture Fight Doesn’t Need a Villain”, was drafted, reviewed and fact-checked by one human and several AI systems before its publication on October 1, 2026, what each step caught, where errors survived, and what the human had to decide. It closes with a checklist for anyone doing the same with their own work. Everything described here is in the working record of drafts, change records and source checks kept for the essay.

The Problem

AI-assisted writing looks finished long before it is right. A draft from a capable model is fluent, well organized and confident, and its errors sit inside sentences that read as well as the true ones. A quotation can be slightly wrong. A source can say less than the sentence claims. A date can be off by a day. None of these announce themselves.

The usual answer is review, and review by other AI systems is cheap and fast. But one reviewer misses things, and several reviewers bring problems of their own. They make mistakes. Their fixes are new text that nobody has checked. Each of them pulls the writing toward its own idea of what a careful document looks like. And when half a dozen systems have touched a piece, it becomes easy to assume that someone else checked the part you didn’t. The question is how to use many checkers without losing track of what has actually been verified, and without losing the piece itself.

What Happened

By its eighth draft, where the surviving record begins, the piece was a Field Note of about 1,750 words, working title “Coupled, but to Whom?”, on the September 2026 argument over whether AI companies’ warnings about extinction are sincere or a strategy to shape regulation in their favor. Its claim was that the question of whom a rule serves can be examined without settling anyone’s motives.

Several systems worked on it, each in a defined role. Claude, from Anthropic, drafted the note in the Institute’s coordinating chat and then worked in three more roles: a separate Claude model reviewed drafts adversarially, mostly in fresh chats, and later drafted the essay version; a Claude chat set up with the Institute’s files gave second opinions and checked sentences against sources, many of which the coordinating instance could not open; and a Claude Code instance coordinated from the eighth draft on, applying fixes and keeping the records. Two GPT models from OpenAI reviewed it: one adversarially, in fresh chats with memory and connectors off, and one constructively, revising the draft to answer the findings. Being from the same company, those two were not independent of each other. Grok, from xAI, ran one of the two early fact checks. The Institute’s founder relayed between them and had the final say.

Over two days the note went through seven versions and grew to about 4,000 words, with a source table covering 48 factual claims. The founder then decided it should be published as a magazine-style essay instead: plain voice, the point early, one argument. The Claude reviewer drafted an essay of about 1,450 words, which went through its own round of checks: a sentence-by-sentence source check, a fresh GPT adversarial review with nine findings, the same reviewer’s confirmation of the fixes, and a pass for voice. On publication day a news check found new developments, and both reviewers read the result once more. The final essay ran to about 2,150 words with 27 sources and was published on October 1 (US time) on synthience.org and LinkedIn, ending with the line “Events as of October 1, 2026.”

Why It Happens

Different reviewers see different things. Different reviewers caught errors the others had passed. The first GPT review found that the note overstated a suspension of service: the source showed access returning in stages, not cut for every user for nineteen days. The Claude reviewer found that no source called the IPO prospectus a “draft”, and that a researcher’s public reply had been described as agreement when it was more qualified than that. The Claude chat found that the note attributed to one critic a claim the critic had not made. None of these was obscure, and three of the four had been marked as verified in the first fact checks; the fourth was text added that same round. A check that a sentence is “verified” means only that someone believed it had been checked.

Reviewers make mistakes too. The Claude reviewer supplied two citations from memory. One quotation held up when it was checked; the other, a paraphrase, did not: the source makes a different point from the one the reviewer remembered. In one confirmation round the review was run on an older copy of the draft, with the reviewer’s connectors on; the record does not say how that happened. And when the same reviewer later drafted the essay, it reintroduced an overstatement it had itself flagged in an earlier round. A reviewer’s finding is a lead to be checked, not a verdict.

Every fix is new text. Each correction adds words that no reviewer has yet read. The coordinating instance’s own additions and changes were corrected by the other checkers at least seven times. A sentence it added about a critic’s views said more than the critic had said. A paragraph it built from secondary reports about a PR firm leaned on a low-quality website and a journalist’s social media post; it was cut back until the founder obtained the report itself, which confirmed part of it. It changed a date to the wrong day and called it the correct date. A sentence it added for balance about two court rulings read as if the rulings conflicted. Later, a sentence it added tied both a demonstration and a report to the same government directive, when the source tied only the report to it. On publication day, it wrote that the Federal Trade Commission had opened an investigation on September 30; both reviewers pointed out that the FTC had confirmed on that day an investigation opened over the summer. A clause about Reuters’ reporting, added in the same round, was dropped because no checker had opened a page that said it.

Review drift. Each review round adds a qualifier, a definition or a citation, and each one can be defended on its own. After several rounds the piece reads like a research paper. The long note grew from about 1,750 words to about 4,000, partly with new reporting and partly with qualifiers. A sentence that read “The text of the rule is enough” became a list of the other evidence to read alongside the rule. The essay drifted too: its subtitle, “Whether AI labs mean what they say about extinction is unknowable. Whom their rules would serve is not,” became “Whether AI labs mean what they say about extinction doesn’t have to be settled before we ask whom their rules would serve.” The fix was right, since “unknowable” claimed too much, but the sentence had lost its force. A voice pass restored it without losing the correction: “You don’t need to know whether the AI labs mean it. You need to know whom their rules would serve.” Elsewhere, “The sequence does not establish anyone’s intent” became “None of it tells us who meant what.”

News goes unnoticed. On September 29, the day before the earliest surviving draft, AI company leaders signed voluntary safety standards at the White House. On September 30 CBS News reported that the FTC had confirmed an investigation and planned to request information from an AI evaluation nonprofit the essay discusses, which the FTC did not say was under investigation. Both bore on the essay’s central questions. The publication-day news check found the FTC item, along with an FTC chair’s remark from mid-September. The White House accord was missed by every round of review and found only when the Claude reviewer read the essay one last time. Even a fixed date can move: one report was dated September 25 in the copy the founder pasted in from Japan and September 24 in the United States, and the timestamp on the publication’s own post announcing it on X settled it, after the note had first been changed to the wrong date.

Risk Surface

The places where errors survived are predictable. The first is any source nobody actually opened. Several sites were blocked or paywalled for one system or another, and a claim can pass several reviews because each reviewer assumes another one read the page. One report could not be opened by the AI systems that tried; the founder opened it and pasted its full text in.

The second is a reviewer’s own interest. The essay discussed Anthropic and OpenAI, and most of the systems checking it came from those two companies. One of them, the Claude reviewer, said so itself and asked that its judgments on the Anthropic passages be checked on another company’s system; two later GPT reviews read the same passages, though neither was asked specifically to recheck that reviewer’s calls. The interest can run either way: the Claude chat found two sentences that leaned against Anthropic. And a Claude reviewer reading text a Claude model drafted is not as independent as it looks. Two reviewers from the same company should not be counted as two independent checks.

The third is late text: the additions made in the final rounds, after most of the reviewing is done, which is where most of the coordinator’s errors were. The fourth is the record itself. Without a written account of which checker opened which source, nobody can say what was verified and what was only read.

Framework Intervention

The Institute’s verification protocols set the method, and the review prompts applied them. The prompts carried the Citation Verification Protocol’s (CVP) evidence standard over to the reviewers themselves: every factual finding had to come with the address of the page the reviewer opened and the exact words on it, in 25 words or fewer, so that each finding could itself be checked. CVP also governed the source table: one row per factual claim, recording which checker opened which source and what it found. Where an original was blocked, a syndicated copy another checker could open was cited instead, and sources of unknown provenance were not used.

Following the idea behind the Ingestion Verification Protocol (IVP), each reviewer first summarized the whole piece, section by section, and the summary was checked for coverage before the review began. That is a light version: IVP itself requires checkpoints through the document with verbatim markers, and does not accept a single summary as proof of reading. Reviewers also got a clean copy of each draft without its internal status line; the first review saw the working copy, and the clean copy was used from the second review on.

The Theoretical Coherence Assurance Protocol (TCAP) supplied the pattern the rounds followed: an adversarial review by a model from another company, a constructive revision that answered each finding and said whether a fix weakened the claim, the human lead’s decision on each, and, as an optional step, a confirmation by the same reviewer that its findings were resolved. Adversarial reviewers were not told that a constructive pass would follow. The review prompts added a rule of their own: in the first review the reviewer did not rate severity, and two other instances classified each finding. Changes between versions were recorded mechanically, paragraph by paragraph, old and new.

Two writing rules came out of this essay and are now part of the Institute’s practice. A reviewer’s fix is applied for its substance, in the article’s own voice, not in the reviewer’s wording. And after each round of fixes, one pass checks voice alone: that nothing has become stiffer, longer or more hedged than accuracy requires.

What Only the Human Decided

The AI systems proposed and edited; the founder made the decisions that shaped the piece. The founder accepted all six findings of the first review and set limits on several: on the suspension, “stay within what the page says.” Of one fix, the founder wrote that it “sharpens the test; it is not a hedge.” The founder decided that length was not a constraint, then that the piece should be an essay, and that it would be published on the Institute’s site and LinkedIn rather than offered to a magazine, since some outlets bar AI-written or AI-edited text and the Institute’s work is disclosed collaboration with AI. The founder opened the report the AI systems could not reach, set the rule that dates in articles follow US time, approved the essay, and decided when the final text went out.

What This Does and Does Not Prove

This is one essay, written under one set of conditions, and it proves nothing general about AI review. It shows that in this case different systems caught different errors, that reviewers introduced errors of their own, that fixes needed checking like any other text, and that the writing stiffened under review until someone checked the voice on purpose. It does not show that more reviewers are always better. For most work, one good adversarial review is enough, as the Institute’s cross-AI review guide says, with one more after major rework. This essay made factual claims about named people and companies in a live dispute, and it got more. The record shows what was caught. It cannot show what was missed.

Checklist

  • Give every checker a defined role, and do not count two models from the same company as independent.
  • Ask for every factual finding with the page address and the exact words, and check the finding against the page.
  • Keep one record of every factual claim: which checker opened which source, and what it says. “Verified” is a claim too.
  • Never rely on a source only one search tool found; have another checker open it.
  • Give reviewers a clean copy without your internal notes, and ask them to summarize the piece first, as a check that they read it all; the review itself should not summarize or rewrite.
  • Ask reviewers to disclose any interest in the subject, and cross-check those calls on another company’s system.
  • Treat every fix as new text, and re-check additions made late in the process, including your own.
  • Apply fixes in your own voice, and after each round read once for voice alone.
  • Before publishing, check the news since your first draft, and put an “events as of” date on anything time-sensitive.
  • Decide yourself what to accept, how far each claim can go, and when to stop.
